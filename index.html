How to play

DefenderCool colours. Walls and one tunable tower type.
AttackerWarm colours. A custom army, a wave you choreograph, bridges and charges.
The KeepThe score. 1,000 points. The attacker wins if it falls.

Play on one device and take turns, looking away while the other side plans. Or play online, each on your own device: you both plan at the same time and the wave starts once both sides are ready. Or play alone against the computer, on either side. Either way you keep your role all match.

  1. The Defender builds in secret, in three steps. Maze: walls (5 coins) bend the route. Towers: place them and tap one to tune it. Ready: check the build and what attacked last time.
  2. The Attacker plans in secret, in four steps. Army: pick units and how many. Wave: arrange when each one leaves the breach on a 20-second timeline. Sabotage: lay a bridge over a wall you’ve seen, or charge towers to knock them out. Ready: check and send.
  3. The wave. Everything is revealed. Bridges go down and charges go off, units take the cheapest route to the keep, towers fire. Tap any unit or tower to inspect it. Afterwards either side can replay it, from the round summary or from the Ready step while planning.
  4. The split. Every round adds a 200-coin pot. The more of the army's strength gets through, the bigger the defender's share, from 30% up to 70%. The side that's losing gets more to recover with.

Controls. Tap or click to build, and drag to draw a line of walls. On touch screens a tower needs a second tap to place, so you can check its range first. On the wave timeline, drag the handles or the dots. When tuning a tower or a unit, the + next to each slider is what the next step up costs; it turns red when you can’t afford it.

The concept

Siegeline is an asymmetric 1v1 PvP tower defense game. One player defends, the other attacks, and each keeps that role for the whole match.

There is no fixed path. The map is contested terrain that both sides reshape every round: the Defender builds a maze, and the Attacker lays bridges through it that stay for the rest of the match, or knocks towers out. Every bridge is ground the Defender can never build on again, so the map fills up with the history of the match. And no two matches start on the same ground: each one scatters rough ground and rock across the board. Neither side picks from a fixed menu either. The Defender tunes every tower and the Attacker designs every unit and choreographs every wave, so each round is a new answer to what the other side did last time. Nobody can settle into one dominant habit, and there is always a move that can swing the match.

Core mechanics

One tower, any build

There is a single tower type. Its tuning starts at 40 coins and every slider or effect multiplies that price. Because the multipliers multiply each other, raising three things at once costs far more than raising each one alone. Range is priced by its square, since a bigger circle covers much more of a winding maze. Fire rate is free: it trades many small hits for a few big ones at the same damage.

Each tower also has a build fee that rises with how many towers you already own: the first is free, then 10, 20, 30 and so on. Walls stay at 5, so by the middle of a match walls are the cheap way to stretch the route.

tower price = 40 × range × damage × splash × frost × poison × chain + build fee

A permanent army, a choreographed wave

The Attacker keeps up to four unit designs. Each design's price is 15 coins times multipliers for health, speed, armor, dodge, regeneration and resistance. The army is like the Defender's towers: coins stay parked in it, every unit marches every wave, and units that die come back for the next one.

The wave timeline decides when each unit leaves the breach. Each design is a track and each unit is a dot; the gap between dots is the gap in time. Bunch a group and it hits the towers in one burst that single-target towers can't handle. Against splash and chain, fewer and tougher units in a looser wave hold up better. Mixing speeds pulls a group apart, so launch slow units earlier if you want everything to arrive together.

unit price = 15 × health × speed × armor × dodge × regen × resist

Above 60 coins a big-unit surcharge applies: 60 × (price ÷ 60)1.5. A fully maxed unit costs about 1,060 instead of 407. One giant unit gives towers only seconds to shoot and nothing for splash to hit, so stacking everything onto it has to cost more.

Regeneration heals 1, 2 or 3% of a unit's health per second, but only once it has gone 3 seconds without a direct hit, and only up to 10, 20 or 30% of its health per wave. It patches units up between towers rather than making them immortal.

Units can also take a resistance that cuts splash or poison damage by 60%, or makes frost 60% weaker. Resistances are never full immunities.

Routes: units dodge tower fire

Units take the cheapest route to the keep. Every cell costs one step, and every working tower that can hit a cell adds half a step more. So units walk around tower range whenever the detour is short, and towers standing on open ground mostly get bypassed. Walls are how the Defender forces the route through the kill zones. The dashed line always shows the route the units will take, and it updates as you build.

cell cost = 1 step + ½ step × towers in range

Terrain: rough ground and rock

Every match starts on a new map. About 5% of the board is rough ground (brown, hatched): units walk across it like open ground, but nothing can be built on it, so it’s a lane the Defender can never wall. About 5% is rock (grey boulders): it blocks units like a wall, and the Defender can build a maze around it for free, but it can’t be removed, built on or bridged.

Both kinds are spread evenly across the board in small clusters of one to three tiles, the gates and the cells around them are always clear, and every map is checked before play: there is always a route, and room for a real maze. Online, both devices build the same map from the match code. A rematch gets a new one.

Bridges, charges and the staged rule

A bridge goes on a wall the Attacker has seen. When the wave starts the wall comes down, its coins go back to the Defender, and the cell is open ground for the rest of the match. Nobody can build on it again. Units treat it like any other open cell, and when two routes cost exactly the same they take the one over the bridge. Towers next to it cover it as usual, and the Defender can wall off either end, as long as a route stays open. Taking the wall down first doesn't stop a bridge. Only a tower built on that cell by then does, because the tower is new: the bridge misses and its coins are lost.

A bridge costs 50 coins plus 20 for every bridge already on the map, and at most 1 can go down per wave.

bridge price = 50 + 20 × bridges on the map

A charge on a tower knocks it offline for one to three waves (25, 55 or 90 coins). An offline tower can't shoot, can't be removed and can't be retuned, but it still blocks its cell. The Defender sees that it's offline, never for how long. No more than half the towers the Attacker has seen, rounded up, can be offline in the same wave, counting towers still offline from earlier charges.

There are no bridges or charges in the first wave, when nothing has been seen yet, or in the final wave: the last round is the army against the defense as built. Both only work on things the Attacker has seen, which means things built before this round. New defenses always get one round of value. Tearing something down and rebuilding it on the same cell doesn't count as new.

Breach, the keep and the shared pot

There is one currency. An army's strength is what it cost. When a unit reaches the keep it carries its cost times the health it has left, so a unit that limps in at 10% health counts for 10%. The breach is the share of the army's strength that got through. It decides two things:

  • The keep loses exactly that much strength.
  • The 200-coin pot is split by it. A clean hold gives the Defender 30% and the Attacker 70%. A 25% breach splits it evenly. A breach of 50% or more gives the Defender 70%.

defender share = 30% + 40% × (breach ÷ 50%), kept between 30% and 70%

Losing a round pays more, so the side that's behind can rebuild. The keep is the real score.

What this demo is

A prototype that proves the core loop, on one screen, across two devices or against the computer. The computer opponent only knows what a human in its seat would know. Each round it tries out dozens of plans in a hidden copy of the wave and plays the one that did best, then explains its choice after the wave. The Attacker always sees the defense as it stood after the last wave, and the Defender sees only the army from the last wave. Online, each side's plan stays private until both lock in; then both devices play the same wave, and the Defender's device settles the result. There is no server and nothing is saved, so reloading the page ends the match. Every number is a rough placeholder, not a balanced value.

Planned tech stack

The full game is a browser game, so it runs on any device and mobile-versus-PC cross-play comes for free. Rendering uses Canvas, moving to PixiJS on WebGL. Multiplayer runs on an authoritative Node.js server over WebSockets. The server owns the game state and only sends each player the other side's moves once both press Ready, so planning is truly simultaneous and nobody can peek.

The static frontend is hosted on Cloudflare Pages or Vercel, and the game servers on Fly.io, Railway or Render. A Postgres database such as Supabase holds accounts, ratings and cosmetics, kept separate from the game servers.

This setup costs close to nothing to start and scales by adding server instances, with no rewrite. The phase-based rounds make syncing simple, and the same architecture can carry real-time mechanics later.

Future directions

  • Separate Attacker Elo and Defender Elo for every player, since skill in one role says little about the other.
  • Matchmaking, lobbies, rankings and leagues.
  • More traits on both sides, each with a counter and a counter to that, so the match plays out as an arms race.
  • Real-time mechanics, such as detonating charges in the middle of a wave.
  • Monetization that is fair to the community: cosmetic only, never pay-to-win.

The guiding rule: a fun game first, a business second.
